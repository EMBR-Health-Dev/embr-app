## How to use this doc

Nothing below goes into EMBR until a menopause clinician has signed it off.
Each section is a draft page with its sources; mark a section approved, or
comment on the line to change.

Rules every page follows:

- EMBR describes and the clinician interprets. No page diagnoses, stages,
  predicts risk or recommends treatment for a specific person.
- Every factual claim cites a peer reviewed paper or a recognised clinical
  body. Blogs, videos and social posts are not sources.
- Nothing on these pages is linked to a person's own data. Education is
  general; the record is hers.
- Plain words, no em dashes or hyphens in brand copy, and an English and
  Japanese version with the same meaning.

## Questions to ask your healthcare provider

A one page printable sheet: short questions grouped by topic, so she can
circle the ones that matter before an appointment. It would sit beside the
BRIEF as a PDF and a page in the app. The questions ask; they assert
nothing.

**About what I have recorded**

- Do the changes in my record fit what you usually see at this stage?
- Is there anything in my record you would want me to track more closely?
- How long should I keep recording before we look again?

**Hot flashes and night sweats**

- Is how often I have them something to act on?
- What options are there, and what would you suggest for me?
- Is there anything I should rule out?

**Sleep**

- Could my sleep problems be linked to the transition, or to something else?
- What could help me sleep better?

**Mood, anxiety and memory**

- Are the mood or anxiety changes I have noticed common at this stage?
- When should I seek more help for mood or anxiety?
- Could anything else be causing the memory or focus changes I have
  noticed, such as my thyroid?

**My cycle**

- Are the changes in my cycle expected?
- Which bleeding changes should I tell you about straight away?

**Treatment**

- Is hormone therapy an option for me, and what are the benefits and risks
  in my case?
- What non hormonal options are there?
- How will we know whether a treatment is helping?

**Work**

- My symptoms are affecting my work. What could help?
- Is there anything you could write down for my workplace, if I choose to
  share it?

**Next steps**

- Do I need any tests?
- When should I come back?
- Who else could help, for example a menopause specialist?

Questions for the clinician reviewer: are any of these leading, missing or
wrong for Japan?

## Understanding the menopause transition

This page explains the STRAW+10 stages in plain words; the app shows the
cycle facts those stages rely on, never a stage. The wording below follows
the STRAW+10 executive summary
([Harlow et al., Menopause 2012](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3340903/)),
checked against the paper.

**Draft page**

Clinicians often describe reproductive ageing in stages, using a system
called STRAW+10. It is based mainly on changes in the menstrual cycle.

- **Early menopausal transition.** Cycle lengths start to vary: the length
  of one cycle differs from the next by 7 days or more, and this happens
  again within the following 10 cycles.
- **Late menopausal transition.** A gap of 60 days or more without a period.
- **Final menstrual period.** It can only be known looking back, once 12
  months have passed with no period.
- **Perimenopause** is the common name for the time from the early
  transition until 12 months after the final period.

During this time hormone levels change and can swing widely. Symptoms such
as hot flashes and night sweats are most likely in the late transition and
the years just after the final period. Only a healthcare provider can say
which stage applies to you, and some conditions and treatments make the
stages harder to apply.

**What EMBR shows in the app** (facts from her own cycle log, no stage named)

| Fact                                 | Example wording                                | Needs                           |
| ------------------------------------ | ---------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------- |
| Variation between consecutive cycles | "Your logged cycles varied by up to 11 days."  | At least 3 logged period starts |
| Longest gap between periods          | "Longest gap between logged periods: 74 days." | At least 2 logged period starts |
| Time since last logged period        | "Last period logged 9 months ago."             | 1 logged period start           |

Each line also says it counts only what was logged, since a missing entry
is not a missing period.

Questions for the clinician reviewer: is the plain wording of the two
transition stages accurate enough, and should the app show these facts in
the BRIEF as well as in the app?

## Anxiety and mood, and when to get help

The urgent help box is the most important line in this whole doc: it should
be live in the app before launch, once reviewed. The page draws on the
Johns Hopkins Medicine article
[Perimenopause and Anxiety](https://www.hopkinsmedicine.org/health/wellness-and-prevention/perimenopause-and-anxiety);
that site blocks automated reading, so the wording below follows the
pasted text and must be checked against the live page.

**Draft page**

Mood changes are common during perimenopause. Some women notice more
anxiety, nervousness or irritability, sometimes at times unrelated to their
cycle, and people close to them may notice it first. Women who have had
anxiety before may be more likely to notice it again during this time.

Anxiety can show up in the body too: a faster heartbeat, sweating, shaking,
digestive problems, disrupted sleep, or finding it harder to focus or
remember things. Hot flashes, night sweats and poor sleep can add to
feeling on edge.

Other conditions, such as thyroid problems, can cause similar symptoms, so
it is worth talking to a healthcare provider rather than assuming the
cause. Help is available, including talking therapies such as cognitive
behavioural therapy, and other options your provider can discuss with you.

**Get help now if** (box shown on the page, and in the app wherever mood or
anxiety is logged)

- you have thoughts of suicide or of harming yourself, or feel hopeless
- anxiety makes it hard to manage daily life, such as washing, eating or
  going to work

Contact your doctor straight away, or call your local emergency number.

## Menopause and the brain

This page is general education on brain fog, sleep and mood, with no risk
figures and nothing tied to her record. It uses only the sources below that
are peer reviewed or from The Menopause Society.

**Draft page**

Brain fog, such as forgetfulness, trouble concentrating and mental
tiredness, is a common complaint during menopause. A 2026 Nature Reviews
Neurology roadmap notes that about two thirds of women report cognitive
concerns during the menopausal transition, and that science still
understands little about how the transition affects the brain
([Nature Reviews Neurology, 2026](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41582-026-01244-5)).

Brain imaging studies have reported differences in brain volume after
menopause, most often in areas linked to memory and thinking
([structured review of 14 MRI studies](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10561270/);
[The Menopause Society, 2025](https://menopause.org/press-releases/how-menopause-restructures-a-womans-brain)).
These are findings across groups of women, not a measure of any one person.

If memory, focus or mood changes worry you, they are worth raising with a
healthcare provider, who can look at the whole picture, including other
possible causes.

**Left out on purpose**

- Dementia, Alzheimer's and cardiovascular risk figures. They are
  population findings that read as personal predictions inside a symptom
  app, which EMBR does not make.
- Overlap with Parkinson's disease and multiple sclerosis. Its only source
  was a video.
- Mechanism detail (oestrogen, BDNF, blood flow). It belongs on a clinician
  written page, if anywhere.

Questions for the clinician reviewer: is a page on this topic useful at all
before launch, or should it wait?

## How EMBR uses research

The PRISMA passage that was shared is the methods section of someone else's
paper, a systematic review by other researchers
([PMC11721711](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11721711/)).
Published as is, it would claim EMBR ran that review. Here is a statement
that stays true, and what makes it true.

**Draft for the website**

> EMBR is built on published research. Every page that explains a symptom
> or a stage of menopause cites peer reviewed studies or recognised
> clinical bodies, and a clinician reviews it before it goes live. EMBR's
> own features count and arrange what you record; they never diagnose or
> predict.

**What makes it true** (do these before publishing the statement)

- [ ] Keep a source log: every claim on every page, its source, and the
      date it was checked. The Sources section below is the start.
- [ ] Name the reviewing clinician and record each sign off with a date.
- [ ] Review each page again at least once a year, or when a major
      guideline changes.

If EMBR later commissions or runs its own systematic review, the methods
can then be described, and only then.
